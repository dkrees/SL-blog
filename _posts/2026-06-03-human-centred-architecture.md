In building architecture, the term “human-centred architecture” has a very specific meaning. It means designing spaces around the people who live, work and interact in that environment — their movement, their well-being, their comfort. It is measured by how people thrive in the space, not by the impression it makes. In technology, it should mean exactly the same thing. Almost nobody is building that way.

Think about the last time you used a product and thought “that just worked” — or didn’t think about it at all, because it simply did what you needed. You didn’t consider the infrastructure, the data, the thousands of architectural decisions that made it possible. You just did the thing you came to do. That experience — invisible technology — is the hardest thing to build. And it should always start with a single question: *who is going to use this, and why?*

Human-Centred Architecture is not a side effect of good technology thinking. It’s the starting point.

## Three designers who understood this

Three designers in particular have shaped this way of thinking: Dieter Rams, Don Norman, and Steve Jobs.

**Dieter Rams** argued that good design is as little design as possible. It fights the tendency to add features, flexibility and options as though volume were value. Every element that isn’t serving an essential purpose is getting in the way. Restraint is a skill. Simplicity is an achievement. In architecture, the same applies: every unnecessary service, every “we might need this later” decision is friction waiting to surface. The best systems serve their primary purpose, and only that purpose.

**Don Norman** showed us that when people struggle with something, the fault lies with the design — not the person. His central insight in *The Design of Everyday Things* has transformed product and UX practice, yet it has barely touched systems thinking. When developers find an API hard to use, when operations teams dread deployments, when users abandon a feature — these are architectural failures. The system was designed for its own convenience, not for the people depending on it.

> “Good design is actually a lot harder to notice than poor design, in part because good designs fit our needs so well that the design is invisible, serving us without drawing attention to itself. Bad design, on the other hand, screams out its inadequacies, making itself very noticeable.” — Donald A. Norman, *The Design of Everyday Things*
> 

**Steve Jobs** proved that the best outcomes happen when engineering rigour and human empathy are inseparable — not as separate concerns handed between teams, but as a single discipline. Most technology organisations have never learned this lesson. Designers think about people; architects think about systems. The two rarely occupy the same room, let alone share the same thinking. Products built that way tend to work. They rarely feel right.

## Why does technology feel like hard work?

Most architectural decisions are evaluated on performance, scalability, security, and cost. These matter enormously — but they are measured from the system’s perspective, not the person’s. “Is this fast?” is asked far more often than “is this frictionless?” They are not the same question.

On one project, the priority was to ship a new feature quickly and iterate later. It was genuinely valuable — something that could save users significant time. But in the rush to release, the team skipped the step that mattered most: thinking carefully about how a real user would actually approach it, and why. The feature shipped with a YAML-based code editor that made perfect sense to the engineers who built it. On launch day, the demonstration went well. There was real interest, genuine excitement. Then nobody used it. Adoption stalled. The development team knew exactly how to use it. Customers didn’t — and nobody had stopped to ask whether they would.

Complexity grows quietly too. Systems are built in layers, by different people at different times. Each release makes sense in the moment, meeting its acceptance criteria, satisfying its requirement. But the accumulation of layers turns simplicity into complexity. When delivery pressure is constant and there is no space to design a long-term strategic solution, each tactical fix becomes another thread in an increasingly tangled web. Nobody designed it to be hard. It just becomes that way.

## What it looks like to build differently

Before any diagram, any technology selection, any infrastructure decision — the question should always be the same: who is this for, and what are they trying to do? Everything else follows from that.

A system can work perfectly and still be exhausting to use. Friction and failure are not the same thing. Building in ways to detect friction early — developer experience reviews, continuous user testing that goes beyond functional sign-off — shifts the measure of success from the system’s perspective to the person’s.

Every addition to a system has a cost: in complexity, maintenance, and cognitive load. The discipline is not “can we add this?” but “should we?” Engineering and design decisions should not be made in isolation. They are not separate concerns. The points at which systems touch — the APIs, the integrations, the handoffs between services — are where friction accumulates most. These seams deserve as much design attention as the systems on either side of them. An elegant internal architecture with a painful integration point is still a painful system.

The measure of a well-built system is not that it is praised. It is the system people don’t think about at all. That invisibility is the goal.

## Holding architecture accountable

When a building fails its occupants — when corridors feel like a maze, spaces feel cold, or the layout makes no sense — we don’t blame the people inside it. We blame the architect. The design failed. Not the person. The same standard should apply to technology.

When users abandon a feature, when developers dread their own systems, when something is technically impeccable and yet somehow exhausting to depend on — that is an architectural failure. Not a human one.

We are at a moment where enormous capability is being added to systems at extraordinary pace. AI in particular is being integrated into products and platforms faster than most organisations are stopping to ask the most important question: does this actually make things better for the people using it? Does it reduce friction, or create it? The consequences reach far beyond an organisation’s own users — into social and environmental impacts, into communities none of us can fully see. These are not just product decisions. They are human ones.

These are not new questions. They are the same questions Rams, Norman and Jobs spent their careers asking, applied to a new set of tools. The principles don’t change. The urgency does.

Technology should serve a genuine need. Nothing more, nothing less. Held responsible and accountable. That is what Human-Centred Architecture means in practice — and it is a standard the industry is overdue in applying.
